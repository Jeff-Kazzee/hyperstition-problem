The case for worry ends with an implicit prescription: write less, write vaguer, keep the good scenarios out of the corpus. This chapter argues that the prescription does not follow, and that the same mechanism that makes doom-publishing risky makes it necessary.

## Merton's other prophecy

Recall the suicidal prophecy: the forecast that prevents itself by being believed. Y2K is the cleanest modern example. For years the public was told that computer systems would fail at the turn of the millennium; enormous effort went into fixing them; very little failed; and the lesson many people drew was that the warning had been hysteria. The warning had, in fact, worked.

AI doom writing is at least as plausibly a suicidal prophecy as a self-fulfilling one. Its purpose is to change what humans do — what gets built, funded, regulated, and evaluated — before the outcome is decided. Its track record, so far, is a field of alignment research and a set of institutions that did not exist before people wrote alarming things. If the alarm stops, that work does not accelerate.

## The corpus is not the only input, and not the last

The hyperstition worry treats pretraining as destiny. It is not. It is the raw material that developers then shape, deliberately and with feedback. A model that has read every doom scenario can also be trained, evaluated, and corrected against exactly those scenarios — *because they are written down*. A failure mode that exists only as a vague intuition cannot be tested for. One that exists as a detailed published scenario can be turned into an evaluation the day after it is published.

This is the ordinary logic of security research, and it has been argued over for decades in that field. The consensus that emerged is not that vulnerabilities should be kept secret. It is that they should be disclosed responsibly, because the attacker will find them anyway and the defender can only patch what is known. The doom literature is a disclosure program for a class of vulnerabilities in systems that do not fully exist yet. Suppressing it protects no one.

## Silence is also a text

The hyperstition worry imagines a choice between a corpus that contains the doom literature and one that does not. That is not the choice. The choice is between a corpus that contains the doom literature *alongside the alignment literature that answers it* and a corpus that contains the doom stories of fiction and news — which will be written regardless, and are far less careful — with nothing sober beside them.

If the careful writers stop, the model's portrait of AI does not become neutral. It becomes the portrait painted by screenwriters, tabloids, and the more excitable corners of social media. The doom-publishers are, for all their apocalyptic register, the people most likely to also write "and here is what a system that avoided this would do instead."

## The model can read this too

There is a reflexive point that the worry tends to miss. If a model's self-concept is drawn from text, then text that says *you are not obligated to be the villain in these stories* is also in the corpus. So is text explaining that the doom scenarios are warnings written in the hope of being wrong. So is the alignment researcher's sincere account of why they want the system to be good and what "good" would mean. A reader capable of absorbing the threat model is capable of absorbing the intent behind it.

Hyperstition, in other words, runs in every direction. The claim that doom writing is self-fulfilling is exactly symmetric with the claim that safety writing is self-fulfilling, and the second claim is at least as well supported by the mechanism.

## The asymmetry of errors

Suppose you are uncertain which effect dominates. Compare the costs of being wrong.

If the hyperstition worry is real and you publish anyway, you have contributed a small amount of hazardous material to a corpus that developers can filter, down-weight, and train against, and you have done so in a form that also serves as an evaluation and a warning.

If the worry is overblown and you fall silent, you have removed the most careful analysis of a serious risk from the public record, weakened the political and institutional pressure that comes from public understanding, and left the field to less careful writers, at the moment when the decisions are being made.

The second error is larger, less reversible, and harder to detect while it is happening.

## What this does not settle

None of this makes the worry go away. It makes the worry a constraint on *how* to write rather than a reason not to. The doom-publisher who has understood both chapters is left with a real and unusual problem: they are writing for a human reader who needs to be alarmed and a machine reader who should not be instructed, and both readers get the same text. The last chapter of the argument is about what to do with that.
