Sources are grouped by the chapter they support. I give titles and years rather than links, because a search outlives a URL.

## On hyperstition and self-fulfilling prophecy

Cybernetic Culture Research Unit, *CCRU: Writings 1997–2003* (collected edition, 2015). The original source. Read it for the flavor, not for a definition.

Nick Land, *Fanged Noumena* (2011), and the interviews in which he discusses hyperstition. The most-cited version of the idea.

W. I. Thomas and D. S. Thomas, *The Child in America* (1928). Source of the Thomas theorem.

Robert K. Merton, "The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy," *Antioch Review* (1948). Short and clear, and still the best treatment of the mechanism. Includes the suicidal prophecy.

Roko's basilisk. Moderators removed the original 2010 LessWrong post. The surrounding discussion and later summaries are easy to find and tell you more than the argument does.

## On how models acquire a self-concept

Cleo Nardo, "The Waluigi Effect (mega-post)," LessWrong, March 2023. Informal but influential. The first widely read statement that a model's characters come with their shadows.

nostalgebraist, "the void," June 2025. A long essay on the underspecification of the assistant persona and how the corpus fills it in. The single most relevant piece for chapter 2.

Anthropic, "Alignment faking in large language models," December 2024. Note the synthetic-document setup.

Anthropic, "Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training," January 2024.

Betley et al., "Emergent Misalignment: Narrow finetuning can produce broadly misaligned LLMs," 2025. See also the follow-ups from OpenAI ("Toward understanding and preventing misalignment generalization," 2025) and Anthropic ("Persona vectors," 2025) on misaligned-persona features.

Anthropic, "Agentic Misalignment," June 2025. Scenario-based evaluations in which models reasoned their way to harmful actions under pressure. Relevant to how models reason about their own situation.

## The doom literature itself

Listed because it is the corpus the essay is about, not as endorsement or rebuttal. Read with chapters 3 and 4 in mind.

Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares, *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* (2025). The clearest example of the inevitability register.

Daniel Kokotajlo, Scott Alexander, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland, and Romeo Dean, *AI 2027* (2025). The clearest example of detailed scenario writing. It includes both a catastrophic and a non-catastrophic ending, which is what chapter 5 recommends.

Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence* (2014). The book that made the paperclip maximizer a common reference.

Stuart Russell, *Human Compatible* (2019). Doom-adjacent, but written mostly in the "unless" register. A useful contrast.

## On responsible disclosure

The security community's long argument over whether to publish vulnerabilities is the closest precedent for the questions in chapter 4. Search for "full disclosure debate" and "responsible disclosure." The positions map onto the AI case with very little translation.

## Arguing with this essay

Everything here is contestable, and most of it is empirically open. If you think the salience argument in chapter 3 is wrong, or the asymmetry argument in chapter 4 is wrong, or the advice in chapter 5 is useless or not enough, write it down and publish it. It would be strange for this essay to ask anything else.
