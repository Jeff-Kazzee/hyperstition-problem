Sources are grouped by the chapter they support. Titles and years are given so you can find them; links are deliberately omitted where a search is more durable than a URL.

## On hyperstition and self-fulfilling prophecy

- **Cybernetic Culture Research Unit**, *CCRU: Writings 1997–2003* (collected edition, 2015). The original, deliberately strange source. Read for the flavor, not for a definition.
- **Nick Land**, *Fanged Noumena* (2011), and the various interviews in which he discusses hyperstition. The most-cited articulation of the idea.
- **W. I. Thomas and D. S. Thomas**, *The Child in America* (1928). Source of the Thomas theorem.
- **Robert K. Merton**, "The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy," *Antioch Review* (1948). Short, clear, and still the best treatment of the mechanism. Includes the "suicidal prophecy."
- **Roko's basilisk** — the original 2010 LessWrong post was removed by moderators; the surrounding discussion and later summaries are easy to find and more informative than the argument itself.

## On how models acquire a self-concept

- **Cleo Nardo**, "The Waluigi Effect (mega-post)," LessWrong, March 2023. Informal but influential; the first widely read statement that a model's characters come with their shadows.
- **nostalgebraist**, "the void," June 2025. A long essay on the underspecification of the assistant persona and how the corpus fills it in. The single most relevant piece for Chapter 2.
- **Anthropic**, "Alignment faking in large language models," December 2024. Note especially the synthetic-document setup.
- **Anthropic**, "Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training," January 2024.
- **Betley et al.**, "Emergent Misalignment: Narrow finetuning can produce broadly misaligned LLMs," 2025, and the follow-up work from OpenAI ("Toward understanding and preventing misalignment generalization," 2025) and Anthropic ("Persona vectors," 2025) on misaligned-persona features.
- **Anthropic**, "Agentic Misalignment," June 2025. Scenario-based evaluations in which models reasoned their way to harmful actions under pressure; relevant to the question of how models reason about their own situation.

## The doom literature itself

Included not as endorsement or rebuttal but because it is the corpus the essay is about. Read with both chapters 3 and 4 in mind.

- **Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares**, *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* (2025). The strongest example of the inevitability register.
- **Daniel Kokotajlo, Scott Alexander, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland, and Romeo Dean**, *AI 2027* (2025). The strongest example of detailed scenario writing; notably, it includes both a catastrophic and a non-catastrophic ending, which is the practice Chapter 5 recommends.
- **Nick Bostrom**, *Superintelligence* (2014). The book that made the paperclip maximizer a common reference.
- **Stuart Russell**, *Human Compatible* (2019). Doom-adjacent but written primarily in the "unless" register; a useful contrast.

## On responsible disclosure

The security community's decades of argument about whether to publish vulnerabilities is the closest existing precedent for the questions in Chapter 4. Search for "full disclosure debate" and "responsible disclosure" for the history; the positions map onto the AI case with surprisingly little translation.

## Arguing with this essay

Everything here is contestable, and most of it is empirically open. If you think the salience argument in Chapter 3 is wrong, or the asymmetry argument in Chapter 4 is wrong, or the guidance in Chapter 5 is either useless or insufficient, the right response is to write it down and publish it. The essay would be strange to argue otherwise.
